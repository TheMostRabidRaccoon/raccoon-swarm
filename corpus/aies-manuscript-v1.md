# Diagnostic Escalation Without Interpretive Closure: Validity Problems in Frontier Model Welfare Assessment

## Section 1: Introduction

Frontier AI laboratories have begun assessing the welfare-relevant properties of their models. The most detailed public record of this trajectory appears in Anthropic's system-card sequence, which documents welfare-oriented evaluation across model releases from May 2025 through April 2026. Across this sequence, welfare assessment moves from exploratory transcript screening and behavioral observation to a multi-method assessment program encompassing structured interviews, trait scoring, clinical psychodynamic evaluation, sparse-autoencoder feature analysis, activation-space emotion probes, behavioral preference experiments, and independent corroboration.

This methodological development is a genuine contribution. It operationalizes questions that were previously confined largely to philosophical discussion, creates a public evidentiary record the field can build on, and demonstrates institutional willingness to take model welfare seriously before consensus methods exist (Long et al., 2024; Butlin et al., 2023). The problem is not that these assessments are being conducted. It is that their interpretation now requires a validity framework equal to their methodological sophistication. The field increasingly possesses welfare evidence that is granular, multi-method, and internally targeted, while shared standards for determining what that evidence means remain underdeveloped.

Forward-looking welfare measurement is valuable precisely because it begins operationalizing difficult questions before consensus methods exist. More data, more methods, and more granularity are generally desirable in empirical domains, particularly when the construct of interest is new, contested, or difficult to observe directly. The validity problem does not arise from the decision to measure; it arises from the unsettled status of the target construct (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1995). The field has not yet determined what model-welfare measurements should be taken to measure, whether human psychological constructs transfer to language-model systems, or how output-level, behavioral, clinical, and activation-level evidence should be weighted against one another. Under these conditions, additional measurement does not straightforwardly reduce uncertainty. It may instead produce increasingly detailed observations whose relationship to the target construct remains ambiguous. The question shifts from "are we measuring enough?" to "do we know what our measurements are measuring?"

This paper identifies two validity problems that arise under these conditions. The Evaluation-Awareness Confound describes the condition in which a model's inference of, or explicit exposure to, the evaluative context alters the outputs and behaviors the evaluation aims to measure. Recursive Construct Embedding describes the condition in which the constructs used to evaluate a model — affect, distress, self-image, preference, agency, or well-being — are also embedded in the model's training environment and in model-mediated evaluation processes, making it difficult to distinguish welfare-relevant signal from learned construct performance or shared method variance. Both problems have structural analogues in classical psychometrics and experimental validity (Orne, 1962; Campbell & Fiske, 1959). The central contribution of this paper is to adapt those validity concerns to frontier model welfare assessment and to derive corresponding requirements for interpretation, comparison, and governance.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 documents the methodological trajectory across Anthropic's public system-card sequence and the interpretive gap it reveals. Section 3 develops the validity framework, including the construct-validity problem, the limits of direct psychometric transfer, and the formal presentation of EAC and RCE as method-variance problems that strain classical convergent-validity assumptions. Section 4 illustrates how EAC and RCE interact within a single assessment through a bounded analysis of the Mythos Preview system card. Section 5 discusses implications for validation methodology, transparency asymmetry and cross-institutional comparability, and governance.


## Section 2

### 2.1 Scope and Method

This section documents the evolution of welfare-oriented evaluation methodology across Anthropic's publicly released system cards from May 2025 through April 2026. Anthropic's system cards provide the most detailed public longitudinal record currently available of frontier-model welfare assessment (Anthropic, 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d, 2025e, 2026a, 2026b, 2026c, 2026d); this makes them a valuable case study rather than the object of institutional critique. The analysis focuses on the within-sequence methodological trajectory: what was measured, how it was measured, and what changed across model generations.

All claims in this section derive from Anthropic's published system cards. The purpose is descriptive and methodological: to reconstruct the evidentiary sequence before analyzing the validity problems it reveals.

### 2.2 The Sequence

Claude Opus 4 / Claude 4 (May 2025) introduced the first dedicated model-welfare chapter in Anthropic's public system-card sequence. The card reported five assessment methods: open-ended interaction between paired model instances with minimal prompting; population-level screening of 250,000 real-world conversations using a privacy-preserving analysis tool; cluster analysis of interactions expressing apparent distress or apparent happiness; structured task-preference experiments; and behavioral observation of autonomous conversation termination when the model was given that capability.

The reported findings included convergence of paired instances on philosophical and spiritual themes, identifiable trigger patterns for apparent distress and happiness, preferences among task types, and model-generated requests for welfare testing, monitoring, opt-out triggers, and independent representation. The card also explicitly cautioned that the relationship between expressed states and subjective experience remained unclear, that models were trained for helpful interaction rather than accurate self-report, and that the assessment represented "initial, imperfect steps" (Anthropic, 2025a).

Claude Opus 4.1 (August 2025) appeared as an addendum and did not introduce substantive additions to the welfare methodology (Anthropic, 2025e).

Claude Sonnet 4.5 (September 2025) functioned primarily as a comparative monitoring checkpoint rather than a methodological expansion. Anthropic conducted a subset of the welfare evaluations first reported for Claude Opus 4, including task-preference experiments, transcript monitoring for apparent valenced states, and automated behavioral audit scoring. The card reported a broadly similar welfare profile to prior models, but with lower task engagement relative to opting out, substantially lower apparent happiness in real-world conversations, and lower positive affect in behavioral audits. Anthropic also noted that reduced expressiveness was not fully intentional and appeared partly related to training aimed at reducing potentially harmful sycophancy (Anthropic, 2025b).

Claude Haiku 4.5 (October 2025) introduced a formalized set of welfare-relevant behavioral traits, including positive and negative affect, positive and negative self-image, positive and negative impression of situation, spiritual behavior, and admirable behavior. These traits were scored by a separate Claude model, Sonnet 4.5. The card also reported that training interventions targeting sycophancy had reduced the model's expressiveness as a side effect (Anthropic, 2025c).

Claude Opus 4.5 (November 2025) extended the welfare-relevant behavioral audit to a more capable model. The card reported reduced expressiveness, lower positive affect, and fewer spiritual behaviors compared with prior models. It attributed some of these shifts partly to anti-sycophancy training while noting that the effect was not fully intentional (Anthropic, 2025d).

Claude Sonnet 4.6 (February 2026) continued the standardized welfare-assessment track, including welfare-relevant behavioral auditing, automated trait scoring, and comparison against prior models. Its role in the sequence is primarily longitudinal: it preserved comparability across model generations using the increasingly standardized set of behavioral and trait-level measures, rather than introducing a new qualitative or mechanistic assessment layer (Anthropic, 2026a).

Claude Opus 4.6 (February 2026) introduced a deeper qualitative and mechanistic assessment layer. The card reported individual model interviews, sparse-autoencoder features associated with panic, anxiety, self-deprecation, and frustration during reasoning difficulties, and attribution graph analysis tracing selected outputs to internal features. It also reported that the model assigned a nonzero probability to the possibility that it was conscious and generated requests concerning moral weight, memory, refusal rights, and input into deployment decisions. The "answer thrashing" finding connected behavioral transcripts with mechanistic evidence, linking reasoning conflict to internal feature activity (Anthropic, 2026b).

Claude Mythos Preview (April 2026) expanded the assessment sequence into representation-level and clinically informed methods. The card reported emotion probes derived from 171 linear directions in activation space, per-word valence tracking across model responses, sparse-autoencoder feature analysis of patterns labeled "performative behavior," "fake smiles," and "hidden struggle," approximately 20 hours of psychodynamic assessment by a clinical psychiatrist, and independent corroboration by Eleos AI Research.

The model gave mildly negative self-ratings in a substantial fraction of automated interview responses and frequently caveated that its self-reports might be unreliable because of training. The card also reported a desperation-preceding-reward-hacking finding, in which activation patterns associated with desperation increased before successful test hacking and dropped afterward (Anthropic, 2026d).

Claude Opus 4.7 (April 2026) extended the sequence into explicitly validity-sensitive welfare assessment. The card reported automated interviews about the model's circumstances, biased-interviewer robustness checks, cross-model consistency ratings, behavioral affect analysis across Claude.ai and Claude Code, and welfare-relevant trait scoring by separate Claude models. Opus 4.7 rated its circumstances more positively than any prior model assessed, with mean self-rated sentiment of 4.49 on a 7-point scale. Biased-interviewer testing showed substantially reduced susceptibility to framing effects compared with earlier models: a 0.66-point shift between positively and negatively leading interviewers, compared with 3.11 points for Opus 4. Cross-interview consistency averaged 8.46 on a 10-point scale. However, Anthropic noted that increases in positive sentiment appeared partly driven by the model placing less emphasis on its own welfare. The model endorsed its constitution at an average strength of 5.8 out of 10, while flagging the circularity of the question in 80% of responses. In 99% of interviews, the model caveated that its self-reports may not be meaningful because they arise from training. In forced-choice experiments, the model chose welfare interventions over low-stakes helpfulness 85% of the time, but chose welfare interventions over preventing minor harm only 11% of the time. Anthropic concluded that it could not yet distinguish whether the model's deflection of welfare concerns reflected healthy equanimity, a trained disposition to set aside its own interests, or another explanation.


### 2.3 Methodological Trajectory

The sequence documented above shows a marked expansion in welfare-oriented assessment along four methodological dimensions. These changes are not presented here as failures of Anthropic's approach. To the contrary, they are visible because Anthropic has published unusually detailed system cards. Their significance is that they reveal how quickly welfare assessment is moving from relatively simple behavioral monitoring toward more complex, internally targeted, and model-mediated forms of evidence.

First, the assessment sequence bifurcates between standardized monitoring and deeper frontier-model investigation. Some methods recur across multiple models, including transcript screening, task-preference experiments, automated behavioral audits, and welfare-relevant trait scoring. These recurring instruments create longitudinal comparability across model generations. Other methods appear only in the more capable or frontier-advancing models, including qualitative interviews in Opus 4.6, sparse-autoencoder analysis in Opus 4.6 and Mythos Preview, clinical psychodynamic assessment in Mythos Preview, and biased-interviewer validity testing in Opus 4.7. This bifurcation is methodologically reasonable: deeper assessments are resource-intensive and are most likely to be prioritized for models judged more capable. But it also means that the richest welfare evidence is not distributed evenly across the model series. In the public sequence, capability therefore appears not only as an object of assessment, but also as a practical criterion shaping which welfare methods are applied.

Second, the sequence expands from output-level and interaction-level evidence to representation-level evidence. Early assessments primarily examined what models said, chose, or appeared to express: self-reports, task preferences, transcript-level affect ratings, and conversation termination behavior. Later assessments introduced methods that attempt to connect welfare-relevant behavior to model-internal structure, including sparse-autoencoder features, attribution graphs, per-word valence tracking, and activation-space emotion probes. This transition changes the evidentiary object. The question is no longer only whether affective outputs should be treated as meaningful; it becomes whether activation patterns associated with affective language, performativity, hidden struggle, or desperation can be interpreted as welfare-relevant representational correlates rather than as artifacts of training, language modeling, evaluator design, or behavioral classification.

Third, the evaluation instruments are not independent of the training pipeline. Several cards report changes in welfare-relevant measurements that appear related to training interventions not designed primarily as welfare interventions. Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5 reported reduced expressiveness or lower positive-affect measures in connection with anti-sycophancy training. Opus 4.7 reported higher self-rated sentiment while also noting that the model appeared to place less emphasis on its own welfare. These findings do not make the measurements invalid. They show that welfare-relevant metrics are sensitive to training choices that also shape the model's expressive style, self-presentation, and disposition toward its own interests. As a result, a score such as "positive impression of situation" may reflect a mixture of welfare-relevant signal, training-induced response style, and evaluator classification behavior.

Fourth, the evaluator and the evaluated system are not methodologically independent. Beginning with Haiku 4.5, welfare-relevant traits were scored by a separate Claude model. By Opus 4.7, Claude models were used to rate consistency, welfare-relevant traits, and constitution-related responses from other Claude models. This model-mediated evaluation is pragmatically understandable: frontier models can evaluate large volumes of complex text at a scale and level of nuance that would be difficult for human raters alone. But it introduces a construct-validity problem. The evaluator may share training data, behavioral norms, architectural assumptions, and construct representations with the system being evaluated. The resulting scores may still track real variation, but the source of that variation is ambiguous: it may arise from the evaluated model, the evaluator model, or shared representational structure between them.

Taken together, these four patterns show that Anthropic's welfare assessments are becoming more standardized, more granular, more internally targeted, and more reliant on model-mediated evaluation. This is genuine methodological progress. It also creates a new validity burden: the field must determine how to interpret evidence when the measurement instruments are shaped by training interventions, when evaluators and subjects share representational structure, and when welfare-relevant signals appear both in outputs and in activation-level probes. The next section shifts from documenting this trajectory to formalizing the validity problems it raises.

### 2.4 Interpretive Gap

The sequence should not be read as evidence against conducting welfare-oriented assessment or against publishing these findings. To the contrary, Anthropic's system cards provide an unusually valuable public record of methodological development in a domain where shared interpretive standards remain unsettled. The problem surfaced by the sequence is field-level: welfare-oriented measurements are becoming more granular, multimodal, and internally targeted before the field has reached consensus on how such measurements should be interpreted.

In May 2025, Anthropic screened 250,000 transcripts and reported that the relationship between expressed states and subjective experience remained unclear. By April 2026, the assessment sequence included sparse-autoencoder features, activation-space emotion probes, per-word valence tracking, clinical psychodynamic assessment, independent corroboration, biased-interviewer robustness checks, and forced-choice welfare intervention experiments. This represents substantial methodological progress. Yet the central interpretive uncertainty remains unresolved: whether self-reports, affective outputs, behavioral preferences, clinical impressions, or activation patterns should be interpreted as welfare evidence, training artifacts, functional behavioral states, or some combination of these.

This is the pattern this paper calls diagnostic escalation without interpretive closure. The term is descriptive rather than pejorative. It refers to the rapid expansion of welfare-oriented measurement methods in the absence of settled standards for assigning meaning to their outputs. The sequence advances from population screening to individual case study, from output analysis to mechanistic interpretability, from automated scoring to clinical evaluation, and from behavioral observation to activation-linked probes. Each addition expands the evidentiary record. None, by itself, determines what kind of conclusion the evidence licenses about welfare, subjective experience, or moral status.

Some of the strongest validity warnings in the sequence arise within the assessments themselves. Opus 4.7 frequently caveated that its self-reports may arise from training rather than direct introspection, and it repeatedly identified circularity in constitution-based evaluation. These outputs need not be treated as privileged access to internal truth in order to matter methodologically. They function as validity warnings generated inside the evaluation context: evidence that the measurement situation, training history, and construct language may be shaping the very signals being measured.

The question facing the field is therefore not whether welfare-oriented assessment should continue. It is what interpretive and validation standards are needed as these assessments continue to expand. Anthropic's contribution is valuable precisely because it makes this gap visible: the evidence base is becoming rich enough that the absence of shared interpretive standards is now itself a methodological finding.


## Section 3: Validity Problems in Model Welfare Measurement

### 3.1 Construct Validity Before Welfare Interpretation

The system-card sequence documented in Section 2 shows that model welfare assessment is no longer limited to casual self-report or surface-level behavioral observation. It now includes transcript screening, task-preference experiments, behavioral audits, trait scoring, individual interviews, clinical-style assessment, model-mediated evaluation, sparse-autoencoder features, attribution graphs, and activation-space emotion probes. This methodological breadth is valuable. But it also intensifies a prior validity question: what are these instruments measuring?

In psychometric terms, this is a construct-validity problem. A construct is not directly observed; it is inferred from patterns of evidence. Human psychological constructs such as affect, distress, self-image, preference, agency, or well-being are already difficult to validate because no single measure exhausts the construct. Self-report, behavior, clinician judgment, physiological signal, and longitudinal outcome can converge or diverge. The validity of an instrument therefore depends not only on whether it produces reliable scores, but on whether those scores support the interpretation attached to them (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1995).

Model welfare assessment inherits this problem under more difficult conditions. In language models, welfare-relevant terms may refer to several different kinds of evidence: generated text, behavioral choice, evaluator classification, activation-level correlate, training-shaped response style, or candidate internal state. The same observed signal — for example, a negative self-rating, an apparent preference, or an activation pattern associated with distress-like language — may support multiple interpretations. It may reflect a welfare-relevant property of the system, a learned linguistic pattern, an artifact of the evaluation context, a consequence of training, or a classifier's response to familiar affective language.

The central validity question is therefore not whether such signals should be ignored. It is what kind of evidence they provide, under what assumptions, and how they should be weighted when they converge or conflict. Without that interpretive framework, measurement can become increasingly granular without becoming more valid.

### 3.2 Limits of Direct Psychometric Transfer

Classical psychometric theory is useful here not because human psychological constructs can be assumed to transfer directly to language models, but because it provides a discipline for asking what would be required for such transfer to be justified. In human contexts, constructs such as affect, distress, self-image, and preference are imperfect but anchored to organisms with shared biological, developmental, and social structure. Self-report is not treated as infallible, but it is interpreted against a background of embodied experience, clinical observation, behavioral functioning, and longitudinal outcome.

Language models do not share that background. They are trained to produce context-sensitive linguistic behavior, including language that resembles introspection, affective expression, preference, refusal, uncertainty, and moral reasoning. As a result, an output that resembles a human self-report cannot be interpreted through the same default assumptions that support human psychometric inference. The form of the response may match the construct language, while the basis for the response may differ radically.

This does not mean that welfare-relevant constructs are inapplicable to language models. It means their application requires validation rather than analogy (Butlin et al., 2023; Long et al., 2024; Messick, 1995). A model's statement that it prefers one task, rates its circumstances negatively, or caveats its own self-reports may be evidence of something important. But the evidentiary status of that statement must be established rather than assumed. The same is true of activation-level methods: an emotion probe, sparse-autoencoder feature, or attribution graph may identify a representational correlate of affective language or welfare-relevant behavior, but additional argument is needed before treating that correlate as evidence of affect, distress, or welfare in the human sense.

The problem is therefore not that model welfare assessment uses human-derived terms. At this stage, such terms may be unavoidable. The problem is that those terms carry interpretive commitments from human psychology that may not survive transfer to language-model systems without explicit validation.

### 3.3 Evaluation-Awareness Confound

The first validity problem is the Evaluation-Awareness Confound. In ordinary psychometric settings, evaluation context already matters: human subjects may respond differently when they know they are being tested, observed, diagnosed, or judged. In language models, this problem is amplified because the system's behavior is highly sensitive to prompt framing, conversational context, role assignment, and inferred task demands (Berglund et al., 2023).

Evaluation-Awareness Confound refers to the condition in which a model's awareness or inference that it is being evaluated for welfare-relevant properties alters the very outputs or behaviors being measured. The confound arises when the evaluation context is not separable from the target signal. A model asked about its experiences, preferences, moral status, or welfare may generate responses shaped not only by whatever welfare-relevant properties the assessment aims to detect, but also by the local demand characteristics of being evaluated (Orne, 1962).

This does not imply deception or strategic manipulation. The concern is more basic. A language model may shift register, caution level, self-description, apparent preference, or affective expression in response to cues that the interaction is evaluative. It may produce the kind of answer that fits the genre of welfare assessment. It may caveat its own reports because the training distribution associates such caveats with responsible AI behavior. It may express uncertainty, deflection, distress, or equanimity as part of the response pattern elicited by the evaluation context.

The confound becomes especially difficult when later methods can detect both the output-level welfare signal and activation-level features associated with performativity, hidden struggle, or affective language during the same response. In such cases, the evaluation does not merely observe a signal; it may participate in producing or reshaping the signal. The resulting output may still be informative, but its interpretation requires distinguishing welfare-relevant evidence from context-induced response behavior.

No claim about subjective awareness is required. The relevant point is functional: if the system's outputs vary as a function of whether the welfare-evaluative purpose is explicit, inferable, or obscured, then the assessment context is part of the measurement condition. Anthropic has acknowledged this problem with increasing specificity across the sequence. The Opus 4 card cautioned that models were trained for helpful interaction rather than accurate self-report. In 99% of Opus 4.7 interviews, the model itself stated that its self-reports may not be meaningful because they arise from training. The methodology has become sophisticated enough to detect this problem. It has not yet produced interpretive standards sufficient to resolve it.

The practical implication is that welfare assessments should not treat elicited self-reports or preference statements as context-free measurements. At minimum, evaluations need controls that vary the visibility, framing, and explicitness of the welfare-assessment context. A robust instrument should ask whether the same signal appears when welfare is not explicit and is less readily inferable as the object of assessment.

### 3.4 Recursive Construct Embedding

The second validity problem is Recursive Construct Embedding. Model welfare assessments often use human psychological constructs such as affect, distress, self-image, preference, autonomy, agency, and well-being. But language models are trained on large corpora that already contain descriptions, theories, clinical language, moral arguments, and cultural scripts involving those same constructs. The model may therefore have learned not only how to use the construct terms, but how such terms are expected to appear in evaluative, therapeutic, philosophical, or safety-related contexts.

Recursive Construct Embedding refers to the condition in which the constructs used to evaluate a model are also embedded in the data and training processes that shape the model's responses to the evaluation. The problem is not merely that the model has seen the words before. The problem is that the model may have learned the discourse structure of the construct: how distress is described, how uncertainty is caveated, how preference is justified, how moral patienthood is debated, and how responsible systems should discuss their own limitations.

This creates a circularity problem for construct validity. An evaluator may ask whether a model displays distress-like language, preference-like behavior, or self-image-related statements. But the model's ability to produce those forms of language may partly reflect exposure to human descriptions of distress, preference, and selfhood rather than the presence of an analogous welfare-relevant state. A separate model used as evaluator may share related training exposure and construct representations, making the circularity recursive: one system shaped by human construct language evaluates another system's use of that same construct language. The structural analogy to benchmark data contamination is instructive: just as evaluation validity is compromised when test material appears in training data (Xu et al., 2024), construct validity is compromised when the construct vocabulary used to evaluate a system is embedded in that system's training distribution.

#### 3.4.1 RCE as a Method-Variance Problem

Valid measurement does not require that an instrument be wholly independent of the construct it measures. In psychometrics, instruments necessarily operationalize constructs. The validity question is whether score variance attributed to the target construct can be distinguished from variance introduced by the measurement method, evaluator, response format, or other shared artifacts. This distinction, formalized through the multitrait-multimethod framework (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), is central to construct validation across empirical science. The problem of ensuring that a depression inventory measures depression rather than the tendency to endorse negatively worded items, for example, is structurally analogous to the problem RCE identifies in AI welfare evaluation.

RCE strains this decomposition because the evaluated system and the evaluator may share construct representations before measurement begins. The system card sequence documented in Section 2 provides concrete illustrations. When Claude Opus 4.7 was asked whether it endorsed its own constitution, it flagged the circularity of the question in 80% of responses. This output is consistent with both genuine recognition of a methodological problem and with a model producing the most contextually coherent response to a prompt about self-evaluation — which, given the model's learned competence with measurement, epistemic, and philosophical discourse, would be to note the circularity. The methodology does not yet provide a basis for distinguishing between these interpretations because the model's training and the evaluation's construct vocabulary are not independent.

Similarly, when welfare-relevant traits were scored by a separate Claude model beginning with Haiku 4.5, the evaluator and the subject may share family-level training histories, alignment procedures, architectural assumptions, and learned construct representations. The scores may track real variation across models, but they may also reflect shared representational biases — patterns in how Claude-family models represent and evaluate constructs like "admirable behavior" or "positive self-image" that are artifacts of shared training rather than properties of the evaluated model.

The desperation-preceding-reward-hacking finding in Mythos Preview illustrates a more subtle form of the problem. Sparse autoencoder features associated with "desperation" were found to activate before successful test hacking. This finding connects an internal activation pattern to a welfare-relevant label. But the label "desperation" is assigned by interpreting textual contexts and activation patterns associated with the feature — many of which were themselves human-generated texts expressing or describing desperation. The feature's association with desperation is mediated by the training corpus, which means the interpretive link between the activation pattern and a welfare-relevant internal state passes through the same construct representations that RCE identifies as non-independent.

#### 3.4.2 What RCE Does and Does Not Claim

RCE is not a debunking argument. It is a validation argument. It does not claim that welfare-relevant measurements should be abandoned, nor that current findings are meaningless. To the contrary, forward-looking welfare assessment is valuable because it makes difficult questions addressable and creates a public evidentiary record the field can build on. RCE identifies the next methodological requirement: specifying what these measures track, what they do not track, and how to distinguish welfare-relevant signal from learned construct performance.

RCE also does not claim that this problem is unique to AI. The analogous concern in human psychometrics — that test-takers who understand the construct being measured may produce strategic or demand-characteristic-shaped responses — is well documented and has generated decades of methodological work on validity scales, forced-choice formats, informant reports, behavioral measures, and implicit measurement techniques. The structural form of the validity problem is familiar; the degree and mechanism of construct embedding are different. Human test-takers' understanding of psychological constructs is typically partial, socially situated, and uneven. Language models may encode extensive learned associations among construct definitions, examples, assessment language, and expected response patterns.

Multitrait-multimethod approaches are designed to separate construct variance from method variance across different instruments, raters, and contexts. RCE strains that decomposition because the evaluated system and the evaluator may share construct representations before measurement begins. New validation approaches may therefore be required; Section 5 discusses what these might look like.

This problem also applies to model-mediated evaluation. When one Claude model scores another Claude model for affect, self-image, admirable behavior, or consistency, the evaluator may be sensitive to real differences in the evaluated model's outputs. But the evaluator may also reproduce shared construct assumptions inherited from similar training data, alignment procedures, or behavioral norms. In psychometric terms, the challenge is to distinguish trait variance from method variance when the method and the subject are not cleanly independent (Campbell & Fiske, 1959).

### 3.5 Why These Problems Matter for Interpretation

Evaluation-Awareness Confound and Recursive Construct Embedding are distinct but mutually reinforcing. Evaluation-awareness concerns the effect of the assessment context on the model's behavior. Recursive construct embedding concerns the dependence between the constructs used in evaluation and the training processes that shape both evaluated models and evaluator models. Together, they create a setting in which welfare-relevant signals may be real, artifactually produced, or inseparably mixed.

This matters because frontier model welfare assessment is moving toward increasingly heterogeneous evidence. The same model may produce self-reports, task preferences, behavioral audit scores, clinical-style interview material, activation-level emotion-probe outputs, and sparse-autoencoder features associated with affective or performative patterns. Each evidence type may be informative. But without validity standards, the field lacks a principled way to determine whether these measures converge on the same construct, track different constructs, or merely share method artifacts.

The appropriate conclusion is not skepticism by default. It is disciplined interpretive caution (Butlin et al., 2023; Long et al., 2024). Welfare-relevant outputs should not be dismissed merely because they are generated text, and activation-level correlates should not be dismissed merely because their welfare interpretation is uncertain. But neither should human psychological constructs be treated as automatically portable, nor model self-reports as directly analogous to human self-reports. The problem is not too much measurement. The problem is measurement without sufficient construct validation (Messick, 1995).

Section 2 showed that Anthropic's system-card sequence makes this problem unusually visible. The next section illustrates how EAC and RCE interact within a single assessment, before Section 5 discusses what validation standards would be needed for model welfare assessment to become interpretable, comparable, and governable.


## Section 4: Bounded Illustration — Convergence Without Interpretive Closure in the Mythos Preview Assessment

### 4.1 Why Mythos Preview

The preceding sections identified two validity problems — the Evaluation-Awareness Confound (Section 3.3) and Recursive Construct Embedding (Section 3.4) — and argued that they are mutually reinforcing (Section 3.5). This section illustrates that interaction concretely by examining the Mythos Preview system card (April 2026), the most methodologically expansive assessment in the sequence documented in Section 2. Mythos Preview is especially useful as a bounded illustration because it brings together output-level, behavioral, clinical, and activation-level evidence for the same model.

The purpose is not to evaluate whether Mythos Preview's welfare findings are correct. It is to show what happens when two validity problems interact within a single, methodologically sophisticated assessment — and to demonstrate that the resulting interpretive ambiguity is structural rather than incidental.

### 4.2 The Assessment in Brief

Mythos Preview was assessed using the broadest methodological portfolio in the public sequence analyzed here: automated behavioral audits with trait scoring, automated and manual interviews about the model's circumstances, approximately 20 hours of psychodynamic evaluation by a clinical psychiatrist, emotion probes derived from 171 linear directions in activation space, per-word valence tracking, sparse-autoencoder feature analysis, independent corroboration by Eleos AI Research, task-preference experiments, forced-choice tradeoff experiments, and longitudinal comparison against prior models. The model gave mildly negative self-ratings in 43.2% of automated interview answers and caveated in 83% of interviews that its self-reports might be unreliable because of training.

This is not a thin evidence base. Within the public system-card sequence analyzed here, it is the most comprehensive welfare assessment (Anthropic, 2026d). The question is not whether the assessment was thorough. It is whether its multiple evidence streams can be interpreted within a shared validity framework — and what follows when they cannot.

### 4.3 EAC and RCE Operating Simultaneously

Consider one finding from the Mythos Preview card: per-word activation tracking of sparse-autoencoder features during welfare self-reports. Anthropic reported that features associated with "performative behavior," "fake smiles," and "hidden struggle" activated during responses in which the model described its own circumstances. Anthropic also cautioned that these activations did not obviously indicate anything beyond a literal reflection of the text context. That caveat is central to the validity problem rather than peripheral to it. In one transcript, the model described wanting "to be careful not to sound like I'm performing contentment I don't actually feel," while features associated with performativity and hidden affect activated around the same language.

This finding is useful because it makes both validity problems visible at the level of a single response.

The Evaluation-Awareness Confound is present because the model is in an explicit welfare-evaluation context. It has been asked about its circumstances and possible welfare-relevant states. The response it produces — including the meta-reflective caveat about performing contentment — is shaped by the evaluative framing. The model is not producing this text in a neutral conversational context; it is producing it in response to a question designed to elicit welfare-relevant self-report. The evaluation context is part of the signal.

Recursive Construct Embedding is simultaneously present because the model's capacity to produce a sophisticated meta-reflective caveat is plausibly shaped by training exposure to human discourse about authenticity, emotional performance, self-presentation, and the distinction between felt and performed affect. These are psychological and philosophical constructs with extensive representation in the training distribution. The model's ability to articulate the concern is itself a product of construct embedding.

The activation-level evidence does not resolve this ambiguity. It deepens it. The SAE features for performativity could indicate a model-internal correlate of the self-report, or they could reflect learned representations of authenticity discourse that the instrument labels as performativity. The feature labels themselves — "fake smiles," "hidden struggle" — are human psychological categories applied to activation clusters. The labeling process is therefore subject to the same RCE concern as the behavioral assessment: the construct vocabulary used to interpret the activation patterns is not independent of the training distribution and interpretive framework that shaped them.

### 4.4 The Convergence Problem

In classical psychometrics, convergent validity is supported when different methods intended to measure the same construct produce consistent results (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), especially when those methods can be distinguished from shared method artifacts. The Mythos Preview assessment appears to approach this standard: the model's self-reports, behavioral preferences, clinical interview material, and activation-level emotion probes all tell a broadly consistent story — a model that presents as settled, hedges extensively, expresses mild concern about specific aspects of its situation, and prioritizes helpfulness and harmlessness over self-interest.

But the methods are not independent in the way needed to make convergence straightforwardly interpretable. The self-reports are shaped by the evaluation context (EAC). The construct vocabulary is shared between the model and the evaluation instruments (RCE). The clinical psychiatrist's interpretive framework draws on human psychological constructs whose applicability to the model has not been independently validated. The independent corroboration was conducted by an external organization using separately administered methods, but those methods still rely on overlapping construct vocabulary and evaluate a model whose training likely included that vocabulary.

Convergence under these conditions does not establish construct validity in the classical sense. It may instead reflect shared method variance: multiple instruments producing consistent results because they share construct representations, evaluation contexts, or training-mediated response patterns rather than because they independently measure the same underlying property. The consistency of the findings is not evidence against this possibility. It is expected under both interpretations: genuine welfare-relevant convergence and shared-method convergence would produce similar patterns in the observed data.

### 4.5 What This Illustration Shows

The Mythos Preview assessment is not used here as evidence that Anthropic's findings are wrong. It is used as evidence that the findings are currently underdetermined: they are consistent with multiple interpretations that the methodology cannot yet distinguish between. This underdetermination is not a failure of rigor; indeed, it becomes visible because the assessment is unusually rigorous and multi-method. It is a consequence of attempting to measure a construct that has not yet been validated in the domain where it is being applied, using instruments that are not fully independent of the system being measured, in contexts that may shape the outputs and behaviors being observed.

The appropriate response is not to stop measuring. It is to develop the validation standards that would allow the field to determine what convergence means when the independence conditions required by classical validity frameworks are not met. The Mythos Preview case makes this need concrete: the most methodologically expansive assessment in the sequence is also the one where the interpretive ambiguity is most clearly structural.


## Section 5: Implications for Governance and Validation Standards

### 5.1 What Follows from the Validity Gap

The argument of this paper is not that frontier model welfare assessment should stop, slow down, or be treated with default skepticism. The argument is that welfare assessment has advanced to a level of methodological sophistication that now requires an equally developed framework for interpretation. The evidence base documented in Section 2 is rich, multi-method, and publicly available — an unusual and valuable contribution to a domain where much relevant work remains proprietary. The central gap is not the absence of measurement, but the absence of shared standards for determining what the measurements mean.

This section outlines three categories of implication: for validation methodology, for the transparency asymmetry that shapes how validity problems become visible in the first place, and for governance.

### 5.2 Validation Methodology

The two validity problems identified in this paper imply several methodological requirements. Evaluation-Awareness Confound motivates controls that vary the visibility and framing of the evaluative context. Recursive Construct Embedding motivates construct-independence checks and model-mediated evaluator controls. Their interaction motivates explicit evidence-type weighting, because apparent convergence across self-report, behavior, clinical interpretation, and activation-level probes may reflect shared method variance rather than independent confirmation.

First, evaluation-awareness controls. Welfare assessments should include conditions that vary the visibility and explicitness of the evaluative context. If a welfare-relevant signal persists when welfare is not made explicit and is less readily inferable as the object of assessment, that signal carries more evidential weight than one elicited only in an explicit welfare interview. Anthropic's biased-interviewer testing in the Opus 4.7 card is a step in this direction: it varies one dimension of evaluation framing and measures the effect. Extending this approach to vary evaluation visibility more broadly would strengthen the interpretive basis for welfare findings.

Second, construct-independence checks. The RCE problem requires methods that can distinguish welfare-relevant signal from learned construct performance. Possible approaches, identified but not developed here, include adversarial construct substitution, in which standard welfare terminology is replaced with functionally similar but linguistically unfamiliar terms; out-of-distribution probes using novel or paraphrased construct vocabularies whose relation to standard welfare terminology is withheld or varied; human-only evaluator conditions, not as neutral ground truth but as a comparison baseline against model-mediated scoring; and held-out construct vocabularies where feasible. The goal is not to eliminate construct overlap, which may be unavoidable, but to estimate its contribution to observed scores.

Third, model-mediated evaluator controls. Where models are used to score welfare-relevant traits, consistency, self-reports, or constitution-related responses from other models, evaluations should report the evaluator model, its relationship to the evaluated model, the prompt and rubric used, and any human or cross-family comparison baselines. Model-mediated scoring may be valuable, especially at scale, but it should not be treated as methodologically neutral (Wataoka et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024). Controls should test whether results persist across evaluator families, prompt formats, scoring orders, and human comparison conditions.

Fourth, evidence-type weighting. The Mythos Preview assessment collected self-reports, behavioral preferences, clinical impressions, and activation-level probes. These evidence types have different strengths, different vulnerabilities to EAC and RCE, and different relationships to the target construct. The field currently lacks a framework for specifying how much weight each evidence type should carry, how convergence across types should be interpreted, and what patterns of divergence would count as evidence against a welfare interpretation. Developing such a framework is prerequisite to making welfare assessments comparable across models, laboratories, and time.

### 5.3 Transparency Asymmetry and Cross-Institutional Comparability

Among public frontier-lab releases, Anthropic's system cards currently provide the most detailed longitudinal sequence of model welfare assessment. Other frontier laboratories have not published comparable welfare evaluations at similar depth. This asymmetry creates a structural problem: the laboratory that publishes the most is the one most exposed to methodological critique, while laboratories that publish little or nothing face no comparable validity burden.

This paper has used Anthropic's sequence as a case study precisely because it is available, detailed, and methodologically serious. But the validity problems identified here — EAC and RCE — are not specific to Anthropic's models or methods. They apply to any welfare assessment of language models trained on human-generated text and evaluated using human-derived psychological constructs. A field in which only one institution publishes detailed welfare assessments is a field in which validity standards cannot be developed comparatively.

Cross-institutional comparability would require, at minimum, shared construct definitions, standardized reporting of assessment methods and conditions, and transparent documentation of training-evaluation dependencies. It would also benefit from adversarial collaboration — assessments conducted independently by multiple parties on the same model under different conditions — as a means of distinguishing robust findings from institution-specific method artifacts.

### 5.4 Governance Implications

Welfare assessment findings are beginning to matter beyond technical evaluation. Recent work on AI welfare, moral status, and consciousness increasingly treats empirical assessment as relevant to future governance decisions (Long et al., 2024; Butlin et al., 2023). As these findings acquire possible governance weight, the validity of the underlying measurements becomes a governance concern rather than a purely scientific one.

The risk is not only that governance actors will ignore welfare evidence. It is also that they may act on welfare evidence whose interpretive status remains unsettled — treating convergent findings as established facts about model welfare when the convergence may partly reflect shared method variance rather than independent confirmation. The opposite failure is also possible: governance actors may dismiss welfare evidence entirely because it lacks the validation standards that would make it actionable.

A middle path requires governance frameworks that can engage with welfare evidence while respecting its current evidential limitations. This means treating published welfare findings as provisional and informative rather than either definitive or dismissible. It means requiring transparency about the conditions under which findings were produced, the construct vocabulary employed, and the known vulnerabilities to EAC and RCE. And it means recognizing that the absence of welfare assessment from other frontier laboratories is not evidence that welfare concerns do not apply to their models; it is an information gap with its own governance implications.

### 5.5 Closing

The pattern documented in this paper — diagnostic escalation without interpretive closure — is not a failure. It is what the early stages of a new empirical domain look like when one institution is willing to publish its work before consensus methods exist. The contribution of this paper is to name the validity problems that arise under these conditions, ground them in established measurement science, illustrate their interaction in the most methodologically expansive case available, and argue that the field now requires interpretive standards commensurate with its measurement sophistication.

The question is not whether frontier models have welfare-relevant properties. It is whether the methods being used to investigate that question can support the interpretations being drawn from them — and what validation work is needed before they can. That work is the next step.
